## What is an Argument

> [!definition] Argument
> An **argument** consists of:
> - **Premises:** Statements assumed to be true (the "given" information).
> - **Conclusion:** The statement that supposedly follows logically from the premises.
>
> The conclusion is typically introduced with $\therefore$ ("therefore").

> [!abstract] Valid vs. Invalid
> | | Definition | What it means |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Valid** | Whenever ALL premises are true, the conclusion is **guaranteed** to be true | The logical structure is correct |
> | **Invalid** (Fallacy) | There exists at least one scenario where all premises are true but the conclusion is false | The reasoning is flawed |

> [!warning] Valid ≠ True
> An argument can be **valid** even if its premises are factually wrong. Validity is about the **logical structure**, not the real-world truth of the statements.
>
> Example of a valid argument with false premises:
> - Premise: All fish can fly.
> - Premise: A shark is a fish.
> - Conclusion: Therefore, a shark can fly.
>
> The structure ($\forall$ fish fly, shark is a fish, $\therefore$ shark flies) is logically valid even though the first premise is false.

## Checking Validity with Truth Tables

> [!tip] The Method
> 1. Write out the premises and conclusion using logical symbols.
> 2. Build a truth table that includes columns for all premises and the conclusion.
> 3. Find every row where **ALL premises are True** simultaneously.
> 4. If the conclusion is **True in ALL such rows** → **Valid**.
> 5. If the conclusion is **False in even ONE such row** → **Invalid**.

### Example 1: Valid Argument (Modus Ponens)

> [!example] Modus Ponens
> - **Premise 1:** $p \to q$ ("If a number is even, it is divisible by 2")
> - **Premise 2:** $p$ ("8 is even")
> - **Conclusion:** $\therefore q$ ("8 is divisible by 2")
>
> | $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | Both premises T? | Conclusion $q$ |
> |:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
> | T | T | T | **Yes** | **T** ✓ |
> | T | F | F | No | — |
> | F | T | T | No | — |
> | F | F | T | No | — |
>
> The only row where both premises hold gives $q = T$. **Valid.**

### Example 2: Invalid Argument (Fallacy of the Inverse)

> [!example] Fallacy of the Inverse
> - **Premise 1:** $p \to q$ ("If interest rates rise, investment decreases")
> - **Premise 2:** $\sim p$ ("Interest rates did not rise")
> - **Conclusion:** $\therefore \sim q$ ("Investment did not decrease")
>
> | $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | $\sim p$ | $\sim q$ | Both T? | Concl. $\sim q$ |
> |:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
> | T | T | T | F | F | No | — |
> | T | F | F | F | T | No | — |
> | F | T | T | T | F | **Yes** | **F** ✗ |
> | F | F | T | T | T | **Yes** | T |
>
> Row 3 has both premises true but the conclusion is **false**. **Invalid!**
>
> Just because interest rates didn't rise doesn't mean investment can't decrease for other reasons.

## Rules of Inference

These are **named argument patterns** that are proven to be **always valid**. You can use them as building blocks for more complex arguments without needing a truth table each time.

> [!abstract] The 7 Rules of Inference
> | Rule | Pattern | Intuition |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Modus Ponens** | $p \to q,\; p \;\vdash\; q$ | "If then, and it is, so it does." |
> | **Modus Tollens** | $p \to q,\; \sim q \;\vdash\; \sim p$ | "It didn't, so it wasn't." |
> | **Addition** | $p \;\vdash\; p \lor q$ | "It is, so at least one is." |
> | **Simplification** | $p \land q \;\vdash\; p$ | "Both are, so each one is." |
> | **Conjunction** | $p,\; q \;\vdash\; p \land q$ | "Each is, so both are." |
> | **Hypothetical Syllogism** | $p \to q,\; q \to r \;\vdash\; p \to r$ | "Chain the implications." |
> | **Disjunctive Syllogism** | $p \lor q,\; \sim p \;\vdash\; q$ | "One or the other; not this, so that." |
>
> The symbol $\vdash$ means "therefore" or "we can conclude."

> [!tip] Recognizing the Rules
> The two most commonly tested rules are:
> - **Modus Ponens:** You have the "if-then" AND the "if" part is true → the "then" part follows.
> - **Modus Tollens:** You have the "if-then" AND the "then" part is false → the "if" part must be false too.

> [!warning] Common Fallacies (Invalid Patterns)
> These look similar to valid rules but are **always invalid**:
>
> | Fallacy | Invalid Pattern | Why it fails |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Affirming the Consequent** | $p \to q,\; q \;\vdash\; p$ | Knowing the conclusion doesn't prove the hypothesis |
> | **Denying the Antecedent** | $p \to q,\; \sim p \;\vdash\; \sim q$ | The conclusion could still be true for other reasons |

## Euler Diagrams

**Euler diagrams** use circles representing categories (sets) to visually test whether categorical arguments are valid.

### Drawing Rules

> [!abstract] How to Draw Euler Diagrams
> | Statement | How to draw it |
> |---|---|
> | "All $P$ are $Q$" | Circle $P$ completely **inside** circle $Q$ |
> | "Some $P$ are $Q$" | Circles $P$ and $Q$ **overlap** |
> | "No $P$ are $Q$" | Circles $P$ and $Q$ are completely **separate** |
> | "$x$ is a $P$" | Place a dot labeled $x$ **inside** circle $P$ |

> [!tip] Testing Validity with Euler Diagrams
> Draw the premises. If the conclusion is **forced** by the diagram (there's no way to arrange things that satisfies the premises but breaks the conclusion), the argument is **valid**.
>
> If you **can** draw a diagram where the premises hold but the conclusion fails, it's **invalid**.

### Valid Argument Example

> [!example] Valid: All CEOs are Business Leaders
> - **Premise 1:** All CEOs are business leaders.
> - **Premise 2:** Elon is a CEO.
> - **Conclusion:** $\therefore$ Elon is a business leader.

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
    % Outer circle - Business Leaders
    \draw[thick, fill=blue!8] (0,0) circle (2.5);
    \node[blue!70!black, font=\small\bfseries] at (0, 2.0) {Business Leaders};
    % Inner circle - CEOs
    \draw[thick, fill=orange!12] (0,-0.5) circle (1.2);
    \node[orange!80!black, font=\small\bfseries] at (0, 0.0) {CEOs};
    % Dot for Elon
    \fill[red!70!black] (0,-0.9) circle (3pt);
    \node[font=\small, red!70!black] at (0.6,-0.9) {Elon};
    \node[font=\small, gray] at (0,-3.3) {Elon is forced inside both circles. \textbf{Valid.}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

Since the CEO circle is entirely inside the Business Leaders circle, placing Elon inside CEOs automatically places him inside Business Leaders. The conclusion is **guaranteed**. **Valid.**

### Invalid Argument Example

> [!example] Invalid: Some Accountants are Auditors
> - **Premise 1:** Some accountants are auditors.
> - **Premise 2:** Maria is an accountant.
> - **Conclusion:** $\therefore$ Maria is an auditor.

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
    % Accountants circle
    \draw[thick, fill=blue!8] (-1,0) circle (1.8);
    \node[blue!70!black, font=\small\bfseries] at (-1, 1.5) {Accountants};
    % Auditors circle
    \draw[thick, fill=orange!12] (1,0) circle (1.8);
    \node[orange!80!black, font=\small\bfseries] at (1, 1.5) {Auditors};
    % Dot for Maria - placed outside the overlap
    \fill[red!70!black] (-2.0, 0) circle (3pt);
    \node[font=\small, red!70!black] at (-2.0, -0.35) {Maria};
    \node[font=\footnotesize, gray] at (-2.0, -0.7) {(could be here!)};
    % Alternative position in overlap
    \draw[dashed, red!40, ->] (-1.85, 0.1) .. controls (-0.5, 0.8) .. (0, 0.1);
    \node[font=\footnotesize, red!40] at (-0.3, 1.0) {or here?};
    \node[font=\small, gray] at (0,-2.6) {Maria could be outside the overlap. \textbf{Invalid.}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

Maria can be placed inside the Accountants circle but **outside** the overlap with Auditors. The premise "some accountants are auditors" is still satisfied (the overlap region exists), but the conclusion that Maria specifically is an auditor is not guaranteed. **Invalid.**

### Another Valid Example

> [!example] Valid: Nurses and Healthcare Workers
> - **Premise 1:** All nurses are healthcare workers.
> - **Premise 2:** Not all healthcare workers are nurses.
> - **Conclusion:** $\therefore$ Some healthcare workers are not nurses.

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
    % Outer circle - Healthcare Workers
    \draw[thick, fill=green!8] (0,0) circle (2.5);
    \node[green!50!black, font=\small\bfseries] at (0, 2.0) {Healthcare Workers};
    % Inner circle - Nurses
    \draw[thick, fill=blue!12] (0,-0.5) circle (1.2);
    \node[blue!70!black, font=\small\bfseries] at (0, 0.0) {Nurses};
    % Shade region that is HW but not Nurses
    \node[font=\small, gray] at (1.7, 0.5) {HW but};
    \node[font=\small, gray] at (1.7, 0.1) {not nurses};
    \draw[->, thick, gray] (1.4, -0.2) -- (0.8, -0.8);
    \node[font=\small, gray] at (0,-3.3) {The diagram inherently shows space outside Nurses. \textbf{Valid.}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

The diagram shows that the Healthcare Workers circle extends beyond the Nurses circle. Since "not all healthcare workers are nurses" means there are elements in that outer region, the conclusion is forced. **Valid.**
