## Introduction to Logic

So, what is logic? In everyday communication, we're constantly evaluating whether things people say are true or false. Logic formalizes this as it's the science of reasoning. It connects directly to the idea of mathematics as a language.


> [!tip] Why do we care?
> Logic gives us the rules to:
> 1. Think clearly about mathematical statements.
> 2. Prove (or disprove) those statements precisely.
> 
> Once we prove a mathematical statement is **TRUE** using logic, it graduates to become a **THEOREM**. Theorems are the absolute foundation of all mathematics.

> [!example] A Statement Requiring Logic
> *"For every positive integer $n$, the sum of positive integers not exceeding $n$ is $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$."*
> We can't just guess if this is true; we need logic to prove it.

### The Knights and Knaves Puzzle

Logic isn't just for math; it's great for solving puzzles!

> [!example] The Classic Island Puzzle
> **Premise:** On a certain island, there are two kinds of people: **Knights** (who *always* tell the truth) and **Knaves** (who *always* lie).
> 
> **Scenario:** You meet Alice and Bob. 
> - Alice says: "Bob is a knight."
> - Bob says: "The two of us are opposite types."
> 
> **Problem:** Who is who?
> *(Spoiler: We use Propositional Logic to solve this systematically instead of just guessing!)*

## Propositions

The basic building block of logic is the proposition.

> [!definition] Proposition
> A **proposition** is a declarative statement (a sentence that declares a fact) that is either **TRUE** or **FALSE**, but never both.

> [!example] Examples of Propositions
> | Statement | Truth Value |
> |---|:---:|
> | Davao City is the capital of Davao del Sur. | ❌ False |
> | Water froze this morning. | ✅ True or ❌ False (depends on context, but it *has* a definite value) |
> | $1 + 1 = 2$ | ✅ True |

> [!warning] These are NOT Propositions
> If it's a question, a command, an exclamation, or has an unknown variable, it's out.
> 1. "What time is it?" $\rightarrow$ *Question*
> 2. $x + 1 = 2$ $\rightarrow$ *Ambiguous (depends on what $x$ is)*
> 3. "Send us your resume before 1 pm." $\rightarrow$ *Command*
> 4. "Fetch my umbrella!" $\rightarrow$ *Exclamation/Command*

### Seatwork: Recognizing Statements

Let's test it out. Are these propositions?

| Statement | Is it a Proposition? | Why? / Truth Value |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| 1. London is in Denmark. | **Yes** | ❌ False |
| 2. Do your homework. | **No** | It's a command |
| 3. NSM wins the match by 2 runs. | **Yes** | ✅ True or ❌ False |
| 4. $x$ is an even number. | **No** | Depends on $x$ |

| 9. The only odd prime number is 2. | **Yes** | ❌ False |
| 10. God bless you! | **No** | Exclamation |

## Propositional Variables

Writing out full sentences gets tedious, so we use variables.

> [!info] Propositional Variables
> We use letters like $p, q, r, s$ to represent entire propositions. This whole system is called **Propositional Logic** (or Sentential Logic).

> [!example] Using Variables
> Let $p$ = "Adam is good at playing Basketball."
> Let $q$ = "Adam is representing the city at the National Level."
> 
> We can combine them: "$p$ **and** $q$" means "Adam is good at playing Basketball AND he is representing the city at the National Level."

## Logical Operators (Connectives)

How do we combine simple propositions into complex ones? We use logical operators (also called connectives). There are 6 main ones you need to know.

### Negation (NOT)

> [!definition] Negation ($\sim p$ or $\neg p$)
> "It is not the case that $p$" or simply "not $p$".
> **Rule:** It just flips the truth value. True becomes False, False becomes True.

| $p$ | $\sim p$ |
| :---: | :---: |
| T | F |
| F | T |

> [!example] 
> - **$p$:** "Mike's PC runs Linux."
> - **$\sim p$:** "Mike's PC does not run Linux."

### Conjunction (AND)

> [!definition] Conjunction ($p \land q$)
> "$p$ and $q$".
> **Rule:** It is a strict operator. It's ONLY true when **both** $p$ and $q$ are true.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \land q$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| T | T | **T** |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | F |
| F | F | F |

### Disjunction (OR)

> [!definition] Disjunction ($p \lor q$)
> "$p$ or $q$".
> **Rule:** It is a chill operator. It's true as long as **at least one** of them is true. It's only false if they are both false.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \lor q$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| T | T | T |
| T | F | T |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | **F** |

### Exclusive Or (XOR)

> [!definition] Exclusive Or ($p \oplus q$)
> "$p$ or $q$, but NOT both".
> **Rule:** It's true when **exactly one** is true. If they match (both true or both false), it's false.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \oplus q$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| T | T | F |
| T | F | T |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | F |

### Conditional (IF-THEN)

> [!definition] Conditional ($p \rightarrow q$)
> "If $p$, then $q$" or "$p$ implies $q$".
> - $p$ is the **hypothesis** (the trigger).
> - $q$ is the **conclusion** (the result).
> **Rule:** It is ONLY false when a true hypothesis leads to a false conclusion (T $\rightarrow$ F). Think of it like a broken promise.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \rightarrow q$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| T | T | T |
| T | F | **F** |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | T |

> [!info] The 4 Forms of a Conditional
> If you start with $p \rightarrow q$, you can rearrange it into three other related statements:
> 1. **Conditional:** $p \rightarrow q$ (If raining, then home team wins)
> 2. **Converse:** $q \rightarrow p$ (If home team wins, then it is raining)
> 3. **Inverse:** $\sim p \rightarrow \sim q$ (If not raining, then home team doesn't win)
> 4. **Contrapositive:** $\sim q \rightarrow \sim p$ (If home team doesn't win, then it's not raining)
> 
> *(Fun fact: The Conditional and the Contrapositive always have the exact same truth value!)*

### Biconditional (IFF)

> [!definition] Biconditional ($p \leftrightarrow q$)
> "$p$ if and only if $q$" (often abbreviated as *iff*).
> **Rule:** It's true when $p$ and $q$ are in perfect agreement (they have the **same truth value**).

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \leftrightarrow q$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | F |
| F | F | T |

## Compound Propositions and Truth Tables

When we start chaining these operators together, we get a **Propositional Form** (a complex expression made of variables and connectives). To figure out if the whole thing is true or false, we build a truth table step-by-step.

> [!example] Example 1: Building a table for $(p \lor q) \land \sim(p \land q)$
> *(By the way, this exact formula is the long way of writing Exclusive OR!)*
> 
> | $p$ | $q$ | $p \lor q$ | $p \land q$ | $\sim(p \land q)$ | $(p \lor q) \land \sim(p \land q)$ |
> |:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
> | T | T | T | T | F | **F** |
> | T | F | T | F | T | **T** |
> | F | T | T | F | T | **T** |
> | F | F | F | F | T | **F** |

> [!example] Example 2: Constructing a Truth Table for $\sim p \lor q$
> Work from the inside out. First find $\sim p$, then OR it with $q$.
> 
> | $p$ | $q$ | $\sim p$ | $\sim p \lor q$ |
> |:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
> | T | T | F | **T** |
> | T | F | F | **F** |
> | F | T | T | **T** |
> | F | F | T | **T** |

> [!example] Example 3: Constructing a Truth Table for $\sim p \land q$
> First find the negation of $p$, then AND it with $q$.
> 
> | $p$ | $q$ | $\sim p$ | $\sim p \land q$ |
> |:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
> | T | T | F | **F** |
> | T | F | F | **F** |
> | F | T | T | **T** |
> | F | F | T | **F** |

> [!example] Example 4: Constructing a Truth Table for $\sim(p \rightarrow \sim q)$
> Work inside the parentheses first. Find $\sim q$, then evaluate the conditional $p \rightarrow \sim q$, and finally negate the whole thing.
> 
> | $p$ | $q$ | $\sim q$ | $p \rightarrow \sim q$ | $\sim(p \rightarrow \sim q)$ |
> |:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
> | T | T | F | F | **T** |
> | T | F | T | T | **F** |
> | F | T | F | T | **F** |
> | F | F | T | T | **F** |

> [!example] Example 5: A Three-Variable Truth Table for $p \lor (q \rightarrow r)$
> With 3 variables ($p, q, r$), we need $2^3 = 8$ rows to cover all possible combinations.
> 
> | $p$ | $q$ | $r$ | $q \rightarrow r$ | $p \lor (q \rightarrow r)$ |
> |:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
> | T | T | T | T | **T** |
> | T | T | F | F | **T** |
> | T | F | T | T | **T** |
> | T | F | F | T | **T** |
> | F | T | T | T | **T** |
> | F | T | F | F | **F** |
> | F | F | T | T | **T** |
> | F | F | F | T | **T** |

### Real-World Applications of Truth Tables

We can also use truth tables to evaluate real-world scenarios by plugging in the truth values of simple statements.

> [!example] Example 6: Evaluating a Compound Statement
> **Given:**
> - $p$: You are 18 years old. (Assume **True**)
> - $q$: You are a registered voter. (Assume **False**)
> - $r$: You are a resident of the barangay. (Assume **True**)
> 
> **Statement to evaluate:** $(p \land q) \rightarrow r$
> 
> **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
> 1. Substitute the truth values: $(\text{T} \land \text{F}) \rightarrow \text{T}$
> 2. Evaluate the parentheses (AND): $\text{T} \land \text{F}$ is **F**.
> 3. Evaluate the conditional: $\text{F} \rightarrow \text{T}$ is **T**.
> 
> **Final Answer:** The compound statement is **True**.

> [!example] Example 7: Evaluating Another Compound Statement
> **Given:**
> - $p$: There is a typhoon signal warning. (Assume **True**)
> - $q$: You have enough food supplies. (Assume **False**)
> - $r$: Your house is in a flood-prone area. (Assume **True**)
> 
> **Statement to evaluate:** $(p \lor q) \leftrightarrow \sim r$
> 
> **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
> 1. Substitute the truth values: $(\text{T} \lor \text{F}) \leftrightarrow \sim \text{T}$
> 2. Evaluate the parentheses (OR): $\text{T} \lor \text{F}$ is **T**.
> 3. Evaluate the negation (NOT): $\sim \text{T}$ is **F**.
> 4. Evaluate the biconditional (IFF): $\text{T} \leftrightarrow \text{F}$ is **F**.
> 
> **Final Answer:** The compound statement is **False**.
