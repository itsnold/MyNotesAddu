# Part 1: Philosophy and the Self

This section covers the major philosophers who shaped how we understand the self.

---

## Socrates (470–399 BCE)

Greek philosopher. Never wrote anything down. Known through Plato's writings.

> [!abstract] Know Thyself
> Self-examination is the highest pursuit. "The unexamined life is not worth living."

> [!info] The Socratic Method
> Teaching through questions, not lectures. Keep asking "why?" until contradictions appear.
> 
> | Step | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | 1. Claim | Someone makes a statement |
> | 2. Question | Socrates asks for clarification |
> | 3. Contradiction | The logic falls apart |
> | 4. Revised understanding | Better knowledge emerges |

> [!abstract] Virtue = Knowledge
> If you truly understand what is good, you will do good. People only do wrong because they're ignorant.

---

## Plato (428–348 BCE)

Student of Socrates, teacher of Aristotle. Founded the Academy in Athens.

> [!info] Theory of Forms
> The physical world is an imperfect copy of a higher realm of perfect, eternal **Forms**.
> 
> | Physical World | World of Forms |
> |---|---|
> | Many chairs | One perfect "Chair" |
> | Changing, imperfect | Eternal, perfect |
> | Known through senses | Known through reason |

> [!info] Allegory of the Cave
> Prisoners chained in a cave only see shadows on the wall and think that's reality.
> 
> | Stage | Represents |
> |---|---|
> | Prisoners in cave | Ordinary people, ignorant |
> | Shadows on wall | Physical world (illusions) |
> | Escaping the cave | Education, philosophy |
> | Seeing the sun | Knowing the Form of the Good |

> [!abstract] Tripartite Soul
> The soul has three parts:
> 
> | Part | Function | Seeks | Virtue |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Reason** | Thinking, logic | Truth | Wisdom |
> | **Spirit** | Emotions, willpower | Honor | Courage |
> | **Appetite** | Desires, bodily needs | Pleasure | Temperance |
> 
> **Justice** = when Reason rules over Spirit and Appetite.

---

## Aristotle (384–322 BCE)

Student of Plato, tutor of Alexander the Great. Focused on the physical world.

> [!abstract] Virtue Ethics and Eudaimonia
> The goal of life is **eudaimonia** (flourishing, well-being). You achieve it by developing virtues through practice.

> [!info] The Golden Mean
> Every virtue is the balance point between two extremes:
> 
> | Vice (Deficiency) | Virtue (Mean) | Vice (Excess) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Cowardice | **Courage** | Recklessness |
> | Stinginess | **Generosity** | Wastefulness |
> | Self-deprecation | **Honesty** | Boastfulness |

> [!info] Four Causes
> To truly understand anything, you need to know four things:
> 
> | Cause | Question | Example (Statue) |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Material** | What is it made of? | Marble |
> | **Formal** | What is its shape? | Human figure |
> | **Efficient** | What made it? | The sculptor |
> | **Final** | What is its purpose? | To honor someone |

> [!abstract] Potentiality and Actuality
> Everything has **potential** that can become **actual**.
> - An acorn has the potential to become an oak tree
> - A student has potential to become a doctor

---

## St. Augustine (354–430 CE)

Christian theologian, Bishop of Hippo. Combined Christian faith with Greek philosophy.

> [!abstract] Original Sin
> All humans inherit Adam and Eve's sin. We're born with a fallen nature inclined toward evil. Only divine grace can save us.

> [!info] Problem of Evil
> If God is all-good and all-powerful, why does evil exist?
> 
> Augustine's answer:
> 1. Evil is not a "thing" but an **absence of good**
> 2. God gave humans **free will**
> 3. Humans chose to turn away from God
> 4. Evil results from misuse of free will

> [!abstract] Divine Illumination
> Human reason alone cannot reach ultimate truth. God must illuminate our minds.

> [!info] City of God vs. City of Man
> | City of God | City of Man |
> |---|---|
> | Love of God | Love of self/worldly things |
> | Leads to heaven | Leads to destruction |

---

## St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)

Dominican friar. Synthesized Aristotle's philosophy with Christian theology.

> [!info] Five Ways (Proofs for God)
> | Way | Argument |
> |---|---|
> | **Motion** | Something must have started all motion → First Mover (God) |
> | **Causation** | Everything has a cause → First Cause |
> | **Contingency** | Things exist but don't have to → Necessary Being |
> | **Gradation** | Things have degrees of perfection → Perfect Being |
> | **Design** | Natural things act toward purposes → Intelligent Designer |

> [!abstract] Natural Law
> Moral law is built into nature and can be discovered through reason.

> [!abstract] Faith and Reason
> Both are valid paths to truth and don't contradict each other.

---

## René Descartes (1596–1650)

French philosopher, mathematician. Father of Modern Philosophy.

> [!abstract] Methodological Doubt
> To find certain knowledge, doubt everything:
> - Senses can deceive (optical illusions)
> - You might be dreaming
> - An evil demon might be tricking you

> [!info] Cogito, Ergo Sum
> "I think, therefore I am."
> 
> Even if everything else is doubtful, the act of doubting proves that I exist as a thinking thing.

> [!info] Mind-Body Dualism
> | Mind (Res Cogitans) | Body (Res Extensa) |
> |---|---|
> | Thinking substance | Extended substance |
> | Non-physical | Physical, mechanical |
> | Free will | Follows laws of physics |
> 
> They interact through the pineal gland.

> [!abstract] Rationalism
> True knowledge comes from reason, not the senses. Innate ideas exist within us from birth.

---

## John Locke (1632–1704)

English philosopher. Father of Liberalism.

> [!abstract] Tabula Rasa
> The mind at birth is a **blank slate**. No innate ideas. All knowledge comes from experience.

> [!info] Empiricism
> All ideas come from two sources:
> 
> | Source | Type | Example |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Sensation** | External experience | Seeing red, feeling heat |
> | **Reflection** | Internal experience | Awareness of thinking |

> [!info] Primary vs. Secondary Qualities
> | Primary (Objective) | Secondary (Subjective) |
> |---|---|
> | In the object itself | In the observer's perception |
> | Size, shape, motion | Color, taste, smell, sound |

> [!abstract] Natural Rights
> All humans possess inherent rights: **Life, Liberty, Property**

---

## David Hume (1711–1776)

Scottish philosopher. Radical empiricist and skeptic.

> [!info] Impressions vs. Ideas
> | Impressions | Ideas |
> |---|---|
> | Vivid, immediate | Faint copies |
> | Direct sensory experience | Recalled in memory |

> [!abstract] Bundle Theory of Self
> There is no permanent, unified "self." You find only a bundle of perceptions (thoughts, feelings, sensations) in constant flux. The "self" is a useful fiction.

> [!abstract] Problem of Induction
> We assume the future will resemble the past. But this cannot be rationally justified.

> [!info] Causation as Habit
> We never actually perceive causation. We see Event A, then Event B, repeatedly. Our mind forms a habit of expecting B after A.

---

## Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

German philosopher. Synthesized rationalism and empiricism.

> [!abstract] Copernican Revolution in Philosophy
> The mind actively shapes experience. Instead of asking how our knowledge conforms to objects, ask how objects conform to our minds.

> [!info] Phenomena vs. Noumena
> | Phenomena | Noumena |
> |---|---|
> | Things as they appear to us | Things-in-themselves |
> | Filtered through mental categories | Beyond our experience |
> | Knowable | Unknowable |

> [!info] Categorical Imperative
> | Formulation | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | Universal Law | Act only by maxims you could will to be universal laws |
> | Humanity | Treat people as ends in themselves, never merely as means |

> [!abstract] Duty and Good Will
> The only thing truly good is a **good will**. Moral worth comes from intention, not consequences.

---

## Sigmund Freud (1856–1939)

Austrian neurologist. Father of Psychoanalysis.

> [!info] Levels of Consciousness (Iceberg Model)
> | Level | Description | Accessibility |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Conscious** | What you're aware of right now | Fully accessible |
> | **Preconscious** | Memories not currently in awareness | Accessible with effort |
> | **Unconscious** | Repressed memories, forbidden desires | Not directly accessible |

> [!info] Structure of the Psyche
> | Part | Principle | Function |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Id** | Pleasure | Primitive instincts, seeks immediate gratification |
> | **Ego** | Reality | Mediates between id and superego |
> | **Superego** | Morality | Internalized rules, conscience, guilt |

> [!info] Psychosexual Stages
> | Stage | Age | Focus |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Oral** | 0–1 | Mouth (sucking, feeding) |
> | **Anal** | 1–3 | Bowel control |
> | **Phallic** | 3–6 | Genitals, Oedipus complex |
> | **Latency** | 6–puberty | Sexual feelings dormant |
> | **Genital** | Puberty+ | Mature sexuality |

> [!info] Defense Mechanisms
> | Mechanism | What it does |
> |---|---|
> | **Repression** | Pushes threatening thoughts into unconscious |
> | **Denial** | Refuses to accept reality |
> | **Projection** | Attributes your feelings to others |
> | **Rationalization** | Creates logical excuses |
> | **Displacement** | Redirects emotions to safer target |
> | **Sublimation** | Channels impulses into acceptable activities |

# Part 2: Imago Dei

This section covers the theological understanding of human dignity.

---

## What is Imago Dei?

**Imago Dei** is Latin for "Image of God." It refers to the belief that humans are created in God's image and likeness.

> [!abstract] Biblical Foundation
> From Genesis 1:26-27:
> "Let us make mankind in our image, in our likeness... So God created mankind in his own image."

> [!info] Key Implications
> | Aspect | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Inherent Dignity** | Every person has worth simply by being human |
> | **Equality** | All humans share this dignity, regardless of status |
> | **Moral Responsibility** | Humans can discern right from wrong |
> | **Capacity for Relationship** | Humans can relate to God and others |
> | **Stewardship** | Humans are caretakers of creation |

---

## Components of Imago Dei

> [!info] Three Aspects
> | Aspect | Description |
> |---|---|
> | **Substantive** | Specific qualities humans share with God (reason, morality, creativity) |
> | **Relational** | Capacity for relationships (with God, others, self) |
> | **Functional** | Role as stewards and representatives of God on earth |

> [!abstract] What Makes Humans Unique
> - **Rationality**: ability to think, reason, and reflect
> - **Morality**: sense of right and wrong
> - **Creativity**: ability to create, innovate, imagine
> - **Spirituality**: capacity for relationship with the divine
> - **Self-awareness**: consciousness of one's own existence

---

## Implications for Understanding the Self

> [!info] Theological View of Identity
> | Traditional View | Imago Dei View |
> |---|---|
> | Identity from achievements | Identity from divine creation |
> | Worth based on productivity | Worth inherent in existence |
> | Value varies by status | Equal dignity for all |

> [!abstract] Human Dignity
> Because humans bear God's image:
> - Every life has intrinsic value
> - Human rights are grounded in divine creation
> - Respect for persons is a moral obligation
> - The self has transcendent significance

> [!tip] Connection to Philosophy
> Augustine and Aquinas both drew on Imago Dei. Augustine saw the image as our capacity for knowledge and love of God. Aquinas emphasized reason and free will as reflections of divine attributes.

# Part 3: Psychological Perspectives

This section covers the humanistic and biological perspectives on the self.

---

## The Humanistic Perspective

> [!summary] Overview
> The "Third Force" in psychology. Rejects Freud's unconscious determinism and behaviorism's environmental determinism. Emphasizes free will, self-actualization, and the inherent drive toward growth.

---

### Philosophical Foundations

> [!info] Existentialism
> Humans have no predetermined essence. You create meaning through your choices.
> 
> | Philosopher | Key Contribution |
> |---|---|
> | **Kierkegaard** (1813–1855) | Subjective truth, individuals create meaning through choice |
> | **Sartre** (1905–1980) | "Existence precedes essence." We make ourselves through action |
> | **Heidegger** (1889–1976) | Authentic existence through confronting mortality |

> [!info] Phenomenology
> Founded by **Edmund Husserl**. The study of conscious experience as it appears to the individual.
> 
> | Concept | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Intentionality** | Consciousness is always *about* something |
> | **Lifeworld** | Subjective reality as immediately experienced |

---

### Carl Rogers (1902–1987)

American psychologist, founder of Client-Centered Therapy.

> [!abstract] Actualizing Tendency
> An innate drive in all humans toward growth, complexity, and autonomy. Like how a plant grows toward sunlight.

> [!abstract] The Fully Functioning Person
> Rogers' vision of psychological health:
> - Open to experience (not defensive)
> - Lives in the present moment
> - Trusts their own feelings and instincts
> - Lives authentically

> [!info] Conditions of Worth
> From childhood, we learn love comes with strings attached.
> 
> | Pattern | Result |
> |---|---|
> | "I'll love you *if* you get good grades" | Match external standards to get love |
> | Suppress real feelings | Living inauthentically |
> | Internal conflict | Psychological problems |

> [!info] Essential Conditions for Therapy
> | Condition | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Unconditional Positive Regard** | Accept the client fully without judgment |
> | **Empathy** | Deeply understand their internal experience |
> | **Congruence** | Be genuine, not hiding behind a facade |

---

### Abraham Maslow (1908–1970)

American psychologist. Shifted focus from pathology to flourishing.

> [!info] Hierarchy of Needs
> You cannot pursue higher needs until lower ones are secured:
> 
> | Level | Needs | Description |
> |---|---|---|
> | 5 | **Self-Actualization** | Realizing your full potential |
> | 4 | **Esteem** | Achievement, recognition, respect |
> | 3 | **Love/Belonging** | Friendship, intimacy, community |
> | 2 | **Safety** | Security, stability, resources |
> | 1 | **Physiological** | Food, water, shelter, sleep |

> [!abstract] Self-Actualization
> Becoming everything you're capable of becoming. Fulfilling your unique potential as a person.

> [!info] Peak Experiences
> Moments of intense joy, clarity, or transcendence. Everything clicks, time seems to stop.

> [!abstract] B-Needs vs. D-Needs
> | D-Needs (Deficiency) | B-Needs (Being) |
> |---|---|
> | Motivated by lack | Motivated by growth |
> | Reduce tension | Embrace experience |
> | Lower levels of hierarchy | Self-actualization level |

---

### Rollo May (1909–1994)

American existential psychologist. The most philosophical of the humanistic psychologists.

> [!abstract] Existential Anxiety
> Freud saw anxiety as a symptom to eliminate. May saw it as built into being human. You feel anxious *because* you're free.

> [!info] Destiny vs. Fate
> | Destiny | Fate |
> |---|---|
> | The limits within which you operate | Passive resignation |
> | Freedom exists *within* these limits | Denies freedom entirely |

---

### Main Principles

> [!abstract] Anti-Determinism
> Rejects both Freud and behaviorism.
> 
> **The existential gap**: Between stimulus and response, there's a space. In that space, *you* choose.

> [!abstract] Holism
> Cannot chop a person into parts. You're a **whole person** and must be understood as one.

> [!abstract] Authenticity vs. Conformity
> | Inauthentic | Authentic |
> |---|---|
> | Living by others' standards | Living by your own values |
> | Suppressing real feelings | Expressing genuine self |
> | "I should be..." | "I am..." |

---

### The Three Forces

> [!info] Historical Context
> | Force | School | View of Humans |
> |---|---|---|
> | First | Psychoanalysis | Driven by unconscious forces |
> | Second | Behaviorism | Shaped by environmental conditioning |
> | **Third** | Humanistic | Self-determining, meaning-seeking |

---

### Criticisms of Humanistic Psychology

> [!warning] Vague and Hard to Test
> How do you measure "self-actualization"? Critics argue it's too abstract to be scientific.

> [!warning] Cultural Bias
> Assumes individual autonomy is the highest good. Collectivist cultures prioritize family and community.

> [!warning] Naively Optimistic
> Assumes humans are inherently good. What about selfishness and cruelty?

---

## The Biological Perspective

> [!summary] Overview
> Treats psychology as applied physiology. All mental states reduce to brain activity, neurotransmitters, hormones, and genes. Mind and brain are the same thing (monism).

---

### Philosophical Foundations

> [!info] Historical Timeline
> | Thinker | Contribution |
> |---|---|
> | **Hippocrates** | Located mental functions in the brain |
> | **Hobbes** (1588–1679) | Radical materialism. Mental processes are motions in the brain |
> | **La Mettrie** (1748) | Humans are biological machines |

> [!abstract] Monism
> Mind and brain are the same thing. This rejects Cartesian dualism.

---

### Major Figures (19th Century)

> [!info] Paul Broca (1861)
> Studied patient "Tan Tan" who could only say "tan."
> 
> **Broca's area** = speech production (left frontal lobe)

> [!info] Carl Wernicke (1874)
> **Wernicke's area** = language comprehension (left temporal lobe)
> 
> | Area | Location | Function |
> |---|---|---|
> | Broca's | Left frontal lobe | Speech production |
> | Wernicke's | Left temporal lobe | Language comprehension |

> [!info] Sir Charles Sherrington
> Discovered the **synapse** and neural transmission.

---

### Major Figures (Modern Era)

> [!info] Wilder Penfield (1891–1976)
> Mapped motor and sensory cortices. Created the **homunculus map**.

> [!info] Roger Sperry (Nobel Prize 1963)
> **Split-brain research**. Studied patients whose corpus callosum was severed.
> 
> | Left Hemisphere | Right Hemisphere |
> |---|---|
> | Language | Spatial processing |
> | Logic | Holistic thinking |
> | Analytical | Pattern recognition |

> [!info] Eric Kandel (Nobel Prize 2000)
> Showed that learning changes synaptic structure at the molecular level.

> [!info] Joseph LeDoux
> Mapped the **amygdala** and fear circuits.

---

### Main Tenets

> [!abstract] Reductionism
> All psychological phenomena can be reduced to biological events:
> 
> | Experience | Biological Basis |
> |---|---|
> | Love | Oxytocin and dopamine in reward circuits |
> | Depression | Serotonin depletion |
> | Memory | Long-term potentiation in synapses |

> [!info] Localization of Function
> Different brain structures handle different functions:
> 
> | Region | Function |
> |---|---|
> | **Prefrontal cortex** | Executive function, planning, impulse control |
> | **Amygdala** | Fear detection, threat assessment |
> | **Hippocampus** | Memory consolidation |
> | **Hypothalamus** | Homeostasis, hunger, thirst |
> | **Cerebellum** | Motor coordination |

> [!info] Neurochemistry
> Mental states depend on chemical messengers:
> 
> | Neurotransmitter | Role |
> |---|---|
> | Serotonin | Mood regulation |
> | Dopamine | Reward, motivation |
> | GABA | Inhibition |
> | Glutamate | Excitation |
> | Norepinephrine | Arousal |

> [!info] Genetic Predisposition
> | Approach | Method |
> |---|---|
> | **Behavioral Genetics** | Twin/adoption studies |
> | **Molecular Genetics** | Identifying specific genes |
> | **Evolutionary Psychology** | Traits selected for ancestral survival |

---

### Research Methods

> [!info] Techniques
> | Method | What It Reveals |
> |---|---|
> | **Lesion Studies** | Function of damaged areas (e.g., Phineas Gage) |
> | **Brain Imaging** (fMRI, PET, EEG) | Real-time activity during tasks |
> | **Twin/Adoption Studies** | Heritability of traits |
> | **Psychopharmacology** | Effects of chemical manipulation |

---

### Modern Updates

> [!abstract] Neuroplasticity
> The brain rewires itself throughout life. London taxi drivers showed enlarged hippocampi from memorizing city layout.

> [!abstract] Epigenetics
> Environment changes gene expression without altering DNA. Nature works *through* nurture.
> 
> | Factor | Effect |
> |---|---|
> | Stress, trauma | Can flip genetic switches on/off |
> | These changes | Can be inherited transgenerationally |

---

### Criticisms of Biological Psychology

> [!warning] Reductionism Loses Meaning
> Love "is" oxytocin chemically. But love as sacrifice and commitment cannot be captured in a formula.

> [!warning] Determinism and Ethics
> If behavior is biologically caused, what about moral responsibility?

> [!warning] Correlation vs. Causation
> Brain imaging shows *where* activity occurs. Does that activity *cause* the experience?

---

### Biopsychosocial Integration

Modern psychology rarely uses this perspective alone.

> [!info] Three Components
> | Component | Contribution |
> |---|---|
> | **Biology** | Sets parameters (temperament, predispositions) |
> | **Psychology** | Fills content (cognition, learning) |
> | **Social** | Triggers expression (culture, relationships) |

> [!tip] Example: Depression
> - **Biological:** Genetic vulnerability, neurotransmitter imbalance
> - **Psychological:** Negative cognitive schemas
> - **Social:** Childhood abuse, social isolation

---

> [!abstract] Final Review Tips
> - Know the key contribution of each philosopher
> - Understand Imago Dei and its implications for human dignity
> - Compare the three forces in psychology
> - Know Rogers' conditions for therapy and Maslow's hierarchy
> - Understand localization of function and key brain regions
> - Be able to distinguish between the perspectives' views on determinism
