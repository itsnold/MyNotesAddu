# The Humanistic Perspective

> [!summary] What this covers
> The "Third Force" in psychology that rejects Freud's unconscious determinism and behaviorism's environmental determinism. Emphasizes free will, self-actualization, and the inherent drive toward growth.

## Philosophical Foundations

Unlike other psychological perspectives that tried to escape philosophy to become "hard science," humanistic psychology deliberately embraced continental philosophy.

> [!info] Existentialism
> The philosophy that humans have no predetermined essence or purpose. You create meaning through your choices.
> 
> | Philosopher | Key Contribution |
> |---|---|
> | **Søren Kierkegaard** (1813–1855) | Subjective truth, dread, the "leap of faith." Individuals must create meaning through choice. |
> | **Jean-Paul Sartre** (1905–1980) | "Existence precedes essence." Humans aren't born with fixed natures—we make ourselves through action. |
> | **Martin Heidegger** (1889–1976) | *Dasein* (being-there), authentic existence through confronting mortality and owning your choices. |
> | **Simone de Beauvoir** (1908–1986) | Ethics of ambiguity; rejecting "bad faith" (self-deception about your own freedom). |

> [!info] Phenomenology
> Founded by **Edmund Husserl** (1859–1938). The study of conscious experience as it appears to the individual.
> 
> | Concept | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Intentionality** | Consciousness is always *about* something. Can't reduce human experience to just stimulus-response or neurons firing. |
> | **Lifeworld (*Lebenswelt*)** | Subjective reality as immediately experienced. Basis for Rogers' "phenomenal field." |

## Carl Rogers
**(1902–1987)** American psychologist, founder of Client-Centered Therapy

Originally trained for ministry before switching to psychology. His therapy approach treats clients as equals, not patients to be diagnosed.

> [!abstract] Actualizing Tendency
> An innate, directional drive in all humans toward growth, complexity, and autonomy. Like how a plant grows toward sunlight—you naturally move toward becoming more capable and self-directed.

> [!abstract] The Fully Functioning Person
> Rogers' vision of psychological health:
> - Open to experience (not defensive)
> - Lives in the present moment
> - Trusts their own feelings and instincts
> - Lives authentically instead of pretending

> [!info] Conditions of Worth
> From childhood, we learn love and approval come with strings attached:
> 
> | Pattern | Result |
> |---|---|
> | "I'll love you *if* you get good grades" | Match external standards to get love |
> | Suppress real feelings/desires | Living inauthentically |
> | Internal conflict | Psychological problems |
> 
> Therapy dissolves these conditions so you can rediscover your authentic self.

> [!info] Essential Conditions for Therapy
> For genuine healing to happen, the therapist must provide:
> 
> | Condition | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Unconditional Positive Regard** | Accept the client fully without judgment |
> | **Empathy** | Deeply understand their internal experience |
> | **Congruence** | Be genuine, not hiding behind a professional facade |

## Abraham Maslow
**(1908–1970)** American psychologist, founder of Humanistic Psychology

Shifted focus from "what's wrong with people" (Freud) to "what makes people flourish."

> [!info] Hierarchy of Needs
> You can't pursue higher needs until lower ones are secured:
> 
> | Level | Needs | Description |
> |---|---|---|
> | 5 | **Self-Actualization** | Realizing your full potential |
> | 4 | **Esteem** | Achievement, recognition, respect |
> | 3 | **Love/Belonging** | Friendship, intimacy, community |
> | 2 | **Safety** | Security, stability, resources |
> | 1 | **Physiological** | Food, water, shelter, sleep |
> 
> You won't care about "finding yourself" if you're starving.

> [!abstract] Self-Actualization
> Becoming everything you're capable of becoming. Not about status or achievement, but about fulfilling your unique potential as a person.

> [!info] Peak Experiences
> Moments of intense joy, clarity, or transcendence. Everything clicks, time seems to stop. Maslow saw these as glimpses of what self-actualization feels like.
> 
> Examples: artistic creation, athletic zone, profound connection with nature, spiritual moments.

> [!abstract] B-Needs vs. D-Needs
> | D-Needs (Deficiency) | B-Needs (Being) |
> |---|---|
> | Motivated by lack | Motivated by growth |
> | Reduce tension | Embrace experience |
> | Lower levels of hierarchy | Self-actualization level |

## Rollo May
**(1909–1994)** American existential psychologist

The most explicitly philosophical of the humanistic psychologists. Trained in theology and imported European existential psychiatry to America.

> [!abstract] Existential Anxiety
> Freud saw anxiety as neurotic symptom to be eliminated. May saw it as **ontological**—built into being human.
> 
> You feel anxious *because* you're free. The weight of your own choices, awareness of mortality—that's not a bug, that's a feature.

> [!info] Destiny vs. Fate
> | Destiny | Fate |
> |---|---|
> | The limits within which you operate | Passive resignation |
> | Birthplace, family, mortality | "I have no choice" |
> | Freedom exists *within* these limits | Denies freedom entirely |

> [!abstract] The Daimonic
> The totality of human drives—creative and destructive. Not just the "bad" parts (like Freud's id), but forces that can be channeled for creativity or destruction depending on whether they're integrated.

## Main Principles

> [!abstract] Anti-Determinism
> Rejects both Freud ("prison of the unconscious") and behaviorism ("prison of the environment").
> 
> **The existential gap**: Between stimulus and response, there's a space. In that space, *you* choose.

> [!abstract] Holism
> Can't chop a person into parts (id/ego/superego, stimulus-response). You're a **whole person** and must be understood as one.
> 
> Influenced by Gestalt psychology (the whole is more than the sum of its parts).

> [!abstract] Authenticity vs. Conformity
> Karen Horney's "tyranny of the shoulds"—living by external standards instead of your true self.
> 
> | Inauthentic | Authentic |
> |---|---|
> | Living by others' standards | Living by your own values |
> | Suppressing real feelings | Expressing genuine self |
> | "I should be..." | "I am..." |

> [!info] I-Thou Relationship
> From philosopher **Martin Buber** (1878–1965):
> 
> | I-It | I-Thou |
> |---|---|
> | Treat person as object | Treat person as presence |
> | Analyze, diagnose, fix | Encounter, relate |
> | One-way | Mutual |
> 
> Rogers built his entire therapy on I-Thou: meet the client as a person, not a case study.

## Third Force Context

The humanistic movement emerged in the 1960s as a deliberate counter to the two dominant schools:

> [!info] The Three Forces
> | Force | School | View of Humans |
> |---|---|---|
> | First | Psychoanalysis | Driven by unconscious forces |
> | Second | Behaviorism | Shaped by environmental conditioning |
> | **Third** | Humanistic | Self-determining, meaning-seeking |

Paralleled the broader counterculture movement—civil rights, anti-war protests, rejection of established authority. Humanistic psychology was the counterculture *in* psychology.

## Criticisms

> [!warning] Vague and Hard to Test
> How do you measure "self-actualization"? How do you run experiments on the meaning of life? Critics argue it's too abstract to be scientific.

> [!warning] Cultural Bias
> The whole framework assumes individual autonomy is the highest good.
> 
> | Western View | Collectivist Cultures |
> |---|---|
> | "Be your authentic self" | Family/community comes first |
> | Individual growth | Social harmony |
> 
> Self-actualization might not be universal.

> [!warning] Naively Optimistic
> Assumes humans are inherently good and growth-oriented. But what about selfishness, cruelty, destruction? Freud would call this dangerously naive about human nature.

---

> [!tip] The Bottom Line
> Rogers and Maslow took Kierkegaard's "becoming a self" and Sartre's "radical freedom" and translated them into therapies and research programs. Whether you buy the optimism or not, they gave psychology a focus on human potential, choice, and meaning that still influences therapy, education, and management today.
