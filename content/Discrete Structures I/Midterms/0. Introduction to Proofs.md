## What Are Proofs

A **proof** is a valid argument that establishes the truth of a mathematical statement.

> [!info] Formal vs Informal Proofs
> In practice, we almost always write **informal proofs** - they're shorter and easier for humans to follow:
> - Multiple rules of inference can be combined into a single step
> - Obvious steps can be skipped
> - Rules used aren't always explicitly named
> 
> The tradeoff: easier to read, but also easier to accidentally introduce errors.

> [!tip] WHY should you care about proofs?
> Besides passing this class, proofs have real applications:
> - **Verification** that computer programs are correct
> - **Establishing** that operating systems are secure
> - **Enabling** programs to make inferences in AI
> - **Showing** that system specifications are consistent
> 
> Every time you write a careful argument for *why* your code works, you're doing informal proof.

## Terminology

These terms come up constantly in math and CS. Know the hierarchy.

> [!definition] Proof Vocabulary
> | Term | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Theorem** | A statement that has been *proven* true (using definitions, axioms, other theorems, and rules of inference) |
> | **Axiom** | A statement assumed to be true without proof - the starting assumptions |
> | **Lemma** | A "helper theorem" - a smaller result used as a stepping stone to prove a bigger theorem |
> | **Corollary** | A result that follows *directly* from a theorem with little or no extra work |
> | **Proposition** | A less important theorem (the word is overloaded - it also means "statement" in logic) |
> | **Conjecture** | A statement *proposed* to be true but not yet proven. Once proven, it becomes a theorem. It might also turn out to be false |


> [!tip] Think of it like building a house
> **Axioms** are the foundation (we accept them as given). **Lemmas** are the bricks and beams. **Theorems** are the completed rooms. **Corollaries** are the furniture that comes naturally once the room is built.

## Forms of Theorems

Most theorems you'll encounter have an implicit universal quantifier.

> [!info] Hidden quantifiers
> When a textbook says:
> > "If $x > y$, where $x$ and $y$ are positive real numbers, then $x^2 > y^2$."
> 
> It **really** means:
> > "**For all** positive real numbers $x$ and $y$, if $x > y$, then $x^2 > y^2$."
> 
> The universal quantifier is omitted by convention.

So most theorems boil down to proving something of the form:
$$\forall x(P(x) \rightarrow Q(x))$$

By **universal generalization**, we pick an arbitrary element $c$ and show $P(c) \rightarrow Q(c)$. Then it holds for all $x$.

This means our core task is always: **prove $p \rightarrow q$**.

## Trivial and Vacuous Proofs

These are the "easy wins" - but they come up more often than you'd think (especially in mathematical induction later).

> [!definition] Trivial Proof
> If we **already know $q$ is true**, then $p \rightarrow q$ is automatically true (regardless of $p$).

> [!example] Trivial Proof
> "If it is raining, then $1 = 1$."
> 
> Since $1 = 1$ is always true, the implication is true no matter what.

> [!definition] Vacuous Proof
> If we **already know $p$ is false**, then $p \rightarrow q$ is automatically true (regardless of $q$).

> [!example] Vacuous Proof
> "If I am both rich and poor, then $2 + 2 = 5$."
> 
> Since no one can be both rich and poor simultaneously, $p$ is always false, so the implication holds vacuously.

> [!tip] WHY do these matter?
> They seem silly on their own, but they're actually **crucial** in mathematical induction (Chapter 5). The base case of many induction proofs is a trivial or vacuous proof.
> 
> An implication $p \to q$ is only false when $p$ is true and $q$ is false. In all other cases, it's true.

## Even and Odd - The Definitions You'll Need

Many proof examples use even/odd integers, so let's nail down the formal definitions.

> [!definition] Even and Odd Integers
> - An integer $n$ is **even** if there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = 2k$
> - An integer $n$ is **odd** if there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = 2k + 1$
> 
> Every integer is either even or odd, and no integer is both.

> [!tip] WHY does this matter?
> These definitions give us **algebraic handles**. When a proof says "assume $n$ is odd," we immediately get to write $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$, and then we can algebraically manipulate that expression.
> 
> This is the key pattern: **translate the English condition into an algebraic equation**, then compute.

## Direct Proof

The most straightforward technique. Assume $p$ is true, then use definitions, algebra, and logic to show $q$ must also be true.

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9]
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=blue!8, thick, font=\small] (assume) at (0,0) {Assume $p$ is true};
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=blue!8, thick, font=\small] (work) at (0,-1.5) {Use definitions, algebra, logic};
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=blue!8, thick, font=\small] (conclude) at (0,-3) {Conclude $q$ is true};

    \draw[->, thick] (assume) -- (work);
    \draw[->, thick] (work) -- (conclude);

    \node[font=\footnotesize, text=gray] at (3.5,0) {Start here};
    \node[font=\footnotesize, text=gray] at (3.5,-3) {So $p \to q$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

> [!example] Direct Proof: "If $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd"
> **Proof:**
> 1. Assume $n$ is odd.
> 2. Then $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$. *(definition of odd)*
> 3. Squaring both sides:
> $$n^2 = (2k + 1)^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$$
> 4. Let $r = 2k^2 + 2k$. Since $k$ is an integer, $r$ is also an integer.
> 5. So $n^2 = 2r + 1$, which is odd by definition. $\blacksquare$
> 
> **Pattern:** Unwrap the definition → algebraically transform → rewrap into the target definition.

> [!abstract] Rational Numbers - Definition
> The real number $r$ is **rational** if there exist integers $p$ and $q$ with $q \neq 0$ such that $r = p/q$.

> [!example] Direct Proof: "The sum of two rational numbers is rational"
> **Proof:**
> 1. Assume $r$ and $s$ are rational numbers.
> 2. Then $r = p/q$ and $s = t/u$ for integers $p, q, t, u$ with $q \neq 0$ and $u \neq 0$.
> 3. Adding:
> $$r + s = \frac{p}{q} + \frac{t}{u} = \frac{pu + qt}{qu}$$
> 4. Since $pu + qt$ is an integer and $qu \neq 0$ is an integer, $r + s$ is rational. $\blacksquare$

## Proof by Contraposition

Instead of proving $p \rightarrow q$ directly, prove its **contrapositive** $\neg q \rightarrow \neg p$. These are logically equivalent, so proving one proves the other.

> [!tip] WHEN to use contraposition
> Use this when the direct approach feels awkward - particularly when:
> - It's easier to work with the **negation** of the conclusion
> - The hypothesis $p$ doesn't give you much to work with directly
> - The conclusion involves "not" or "is not" statements

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9]
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3.5cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=red!8, thick, font=\small] (assume) at (0,0) {Assume $\neg q$ (negate conclusion)};
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3.5cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=red!8, thick, font=\small] (work) at (0,-1.5) {Use definitions, algebra, logic};
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3.5cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=red!8, thick, font=\small] (conclude) at (0,-3) {Conclude $\neg p$ (negated hypothesis)};

    \draw[->, thick] (assume) -- (work);
    \draw[->, thick] (work) -- (conclude);

    \node[font=\footnotesize, text=gray] at (4.5,0) {Start from the end};
    \node[font=\footnotesize, text=gray] at (4.8,-3) {So $p \to q$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

> [!example] Contraposition: "If $3n + 2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd"
> **Proof (by contraposition - we prove: if $n$ is even, then $3n + 2$ is even):**
> 1. Assume $n$ is even (i.e., $\neg q$: "$n$ is NOT odd").
> 2. Then $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$.
> 3. Substituting:
> $$3n + 2 = 3(2k) + 2 = 6k + 2 = 2(3k + 1)$$
> 4. Let $j = 3k + 1$. Since $k$ is an integer, $j$ is an integer.
> 5. So $3n + 2 = 2j$, which is even ($\neg p$: "$3n + 2$ is NOT odd"). $\blacksquare$
> 
> We proved $\neg q \rightarrow \neg p$, which is equivalent to $p \rightarrow q$.

> [!example] Contraposition: "If $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd"
> **Proof (by contraposition - we prove: if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even):**
> 1. Assume $n$ is even, so $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$.
> 2. Then:
> $$n^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)$$
> 3. Since $2k^2$ is an integer, $n^2$ is even. $\blacksquare$
> 
> By contraposition: if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.

## Proof by Contradiction

To prove statement $p$, assume $\neg p$ and derive a **contradiction** (something impossible like $q \land \neg q$). Since $\neg p$ leads to nonsense, $p$ must be true.

Also called *reductio ad absurdum* (reduction to absurdity).

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9]
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3.5cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=orange!10, thick, font=\small] (assume) at (0,0) {Assume $\neg p$ (the opposite)};
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3.5cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=orange!10, thick, font=\small] (work) at (0,-1.5) {Use definitions, algebra, logic};
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3.5cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=red!15, thick, font=\small] (contra) at (0,-3) {Reach a contradiction!};
    \node[draw, rounded corners=4pt, minimum width=3.5cm, minimum height=0.6cm, fill=green!10, thick, font=\small] (conclude) at (0,-4.5) {Therefore $p$ must be true};

    \draw[->, thick] (assume) -- (work);
    \draw[->, thick] (work) -- (contra);
    \draw[->, thick] (contra) -- (conclude);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

> [!tip] Contradiction vs Contraposition
> They're easy to confuse. Here's the difference:
> 
> | Method | You assume... | You show... |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Contraposition** | $\neg q$ (negate the conclusion) | $\neg p$ (the hypothesis is also negated) |
> | **Contradiction** | $\neg p$ (the opposite of what you want) | Something impossible / absurd |
> 
> Contraposition proves $p \to q$ by proving $\neg q \to \neg p$.
> Contradiction proves $p$ by showing $\neg p$ leads to nonsense.

> [!example] Contradiction: "22 days → at least 4 on the same weekday"
> **Claim:** If you pick 22 days from the calendar, at least 4 must fall on the same day of the week.
> 
> **Proof (by contradiction):**
> 1. Assume the opposite: **no more than 3** of the 22 days fall on any given day of the week.
> 2. There are 7 days in a week. If each day has at most 3 dates, then we can have at most:
> $$7 \times 3 = 21 \text{ days}$$
> 3. But we picked **22** days. This is a contradiction ($22 > 21$). $\lightning$
> 4. Therefore, at least 4 of the 22 days must fall on the same day of the week. $\blacksquare$
> 
> This is actually an application of the **Pigeonhole Principle** - if you stuff $n$ items into $k$ boxes, at least one box has $\lceil n/k \rceil$ items.

## Biconditional Proofs

To prove $p \leftrightarrow q$ ("$p$ if and only if $q$"), prove **both directions**: $p \rightarrow q$ AND $q \rightarrow p$.

> [!definition] Proving Biconditionals
> $$p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p \rightarrow q) \land (q \rightarrow p)$$
> You need two separate proofs, one for each direction.

> [!example] Biconditional: "$n$ is odd iff $n^2$ is odd"
> **Proof:**
> 
> **($\Rightarrow$) If $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd:**
> We proved this via direct proof (see above). $\checkmark$
> 
> **($\Leftarrow$) If $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd:**
> We proved this via contraposition (see above). $\checkmark$
> 
> Since both directions hold, $n$ is odd $\iff$ $n^2$ is odd. $\blacksquare$

> [!info] Shorthand
> The abbreviation **iff** means "if and only if."
> 
> "$n$ is odd **iff** $n^2$ is odd" = "$n$ is odd $\leftrightarrow$ $n^2$ is odd."

## The Bogus Proof

A classic exercise in spotting errors. Where does this "proof" go wrong?

> [!warning] "Proof" that $1 = 2$
> | Step | Statement | Reason |
> |:---:|:---|:---|
> | 1 | $a = b$ | Premise |
> | 2 | $a^2 = ab$ | Multiply both sides by $a$ |
> | 3 | $a^2 - b^2 = ab - b^2$ | Subtract $b^2$ from both sides |
> | 4 | $(a-b)(a+b) = b(a-b)$ | Factor both sides |
> | 5 | $a + b = b$ | Divide both sides by $(a-b)$ |
> | 6 | $2b = b$ | Substitute $a = b$ |
> | 7 | $2 = 1$ | Divide by $b$ |

> [!success] The Error
> **Step 5** is invalid. Since $a = b$ (given in Step 1), we have $a - b = 0$. Dividing both sides by $(a - b)$ is **division by zero**, which is undefined.
> 
> This is why we always need to check our denominators. Every step in a proof must be *valid*.

> [!tip] Takeaway
> In any proof, **never divide by an expression that could be zero** without first proving it isn't zero. This is a common source of bogus proofs.

## Summary: Choosing Your Proof Strategy

> [!abstract] Quick Reference
> | Method | When to Use | Structure |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Direct** | The most natural approach; $p$ gives useful info | Assume $p$, derive $q$ |
> | **Contraposition** | $\neg q$ is easier to work with than $p$ | Assume $\neg q$, derive $\neg p$ |
> | **Contradiction** | You need to prove existence, or the statement isn't an implication | Assume $\neg p$, reach absurdity |
> | **Trivial** | You already know $q$ is true | Show $q$ is true regardless |
> | **Vacuous** | You already know $p$ is false | Show $p$ is false regardless |
> | **Biconditional** | Proving $p \iff q$ | Prove $p \to q$ and $q \to p$ separately |

> [!tip] General advice
> - **Start with direct proof.** It's the simplest and most common.
> - If that doesn't work, **try contraposition** - especially if the conclusion involves "not" or negation.
> - **Contradiction** is the nuclear option - powerful but sometimes harder to control. Use it when you need to prove something exists or when direct/contraposition feel stuck.
