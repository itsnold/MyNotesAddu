## What is a Function

> [!definition] Function
> A **function** $f$ from $A$ to $B$, written $f: A \to B$, is an assignment of **each** element of $A$ to **exactly one** element of $B$.
> 
> If $b$ is the unique element assigned to $a$, we write $f(a) = b$.
> 
> Functions are also called **mappings** or **transformations**.

```tikz

```

> [!info] Function as a Relation
> A function $f: A \to B$ is a special kind of relation - a subset of $A \times B$ where:
> 1. Every $a \in A$ appears as the first element in exactly one pair
> 2. No two pairs have the same first element with different second elements
> 
> Formally:
> $$\forall x [x \in A \to \exists y [y \in B \land (x,y) \in f]]$$
> $$\forall x, y_1, y_2 [[(x,y_1) \in f \land (x,y_2) \in f] \to y_1 = y_2]$$

## Domain, Codomain, and Range

> [!abstract] Terminology
> Given $f: A \to B$ where $f(a) = b$:
> 
> | Term | Symbol | Meaning |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Domain** | $A$ | The set of all inputs |
> | **Codomain** | $B$ | The set of all *possible* outputs |
> | **Image** of $a$ | $b = f(a)$ | The output for input $a$ |
> | **Preimage** of $b$ | $a$ | The input(s) that map to $b$ |
> | **Range** | $f(A)$ | The set of all *actual* outputs |
> 
> The **range** $\subseteq$ codomain, but they may not be equal.

> [!example] Walkthrough
> $A = \{a, b, c, d\}$, $B = \{x, y, z\}$
> 
> Mapping: $a \to z$, $b \to y$, $c \to z$, $d \to z$
> 
> | Question | Answer |
> |---|---|
> | $f(a)$ = ? | $z$ |
> | Image of $d$ = ? | $z$ |
> | Domain of $f$ = ? | $A = \{a,b,c,d\}$ |
> | Codomain of $f$ = ? | $B = \{x,y,z\}$ |
> | Preimage of $y$ = ? | $b$ |
> | $f(A)$ (range) = ? | $\{y, z\}$ |
> | Preimage(s) of $z$ = ? | $\{a, c, d\}$ |
> 
> Notice: $x \in B$ but $x \notin f(A)$. The codomain has $x$, but no one maps to it.

> [!info] Image of a Subset
> If $S \subseteq A$, then $f(S) = \{f(s) \mid s \in S\}$ - the set of all images of elements in $S$.

## Injections (One-to-One)

> [!definition] Injective Function
> A function $f$ is **one-to-one** (or **injective**) if different inputs always produce different outputs:
> $$f(a) = f(b) \implies a = b$$
> 
> Equivalently: $a \neq b \implies f(a) \neq f(b)$
> 
> No two arrows point to the same target.

> [!tip] WHY "one-to-one"?
> The name makes sense: each output is hit by **at most one** input. One input → one output, no sharing allowed.
> 
> To **disprove** injectivity, you just need ONE counterexample: two different inputs that land on the same output. For $f(x) = x^2$, we have $f(2) = f(-2) = 4$ - done, not injective.
> 
> To **prove** injectivity, assume two inputs give the same output ($f(a) = f(b)$) and show the inputs must have been the same ($a = b$). If you can always force $a = b$, it's injective.

## Surjections (Onto)

> [!definition] Surjective Function
> A function $f: A \to B$ is **onto** (or **surjective**) if every element in $B$ is the image of at least one element in $A$:
> $$\forall b \in B, \exists a \in A : f(a) = b$$
> 
> The range equals the codomain: $f(A) = B$.

> [!tip] WHY "onto"?
> Surjection means the function **covers** the entire codomain - every element in $B$ actually gets mapped **onto**. Nothing is left out.
> 
> If even one element in $B$ has no arrow pointing to it, the function is NOT surjective. For $f(x) = x^2$ from $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$: the number $-1$ is in the codomain but $x^2 = -1$ has no integer solution.
> 
> To **prove** surjectivity: pick an arbitrary $y \in B$ and **construct** an $x$ that maps to it. For $f(x) = 2x + 1$: given any $y$, set $x = (y-1)/2$, check: $f((y-1)/2) = y$. Works!

## Bijections

> [!definition] Bijection
> A function is a **bijection** (or **one-to-one correspondence**) if it is **both injective AND surjective**.
> 
> Perfect pairing: every input maps to a unique output, and every possible output is used.

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
    % Set A
    \draw[thick, fill=blue!8] (-2,0) ellipse (1 and 1.8);

    \node[font=\small] (a1) at (-2,1) {$a$};
    \node[font=\small] (a2) at (-2,0.3) {$b$};
    \node[font=\small] (a3) at (-2,-0.4) {$c$};
    \node[font=\small] (a4) at (-2,-1.1) {$d$};
    % Set B
    \draw[thick, fill=yellow!15] (2,0) ellipse (1 and 1.8);
    \node[font=\small, orange!80!black] at (2,2.1) {$B$};
    \node[font=\small] (b1) at (2,1) {$w$};
    \node[font=\small] (b2) at (2,0.3) {$y$};
    \node[font=\small] (b3) at (2,-0.4) {$x$};
    \node[font=\small] (b4) at (2,-1.1) {$z$};
    % Arrows (perfect pairing)
    \draw[->, thick, gray] (a1.east) -- (b1.west);
    \draw[->, thick, gray] (a2.east) -- (b2.west);
    \draw[->, thick, gray] (a3.east) -- (b3.west);
    \draw[->, thick, gray] (a4.east) -- (b4.west);

```

> [!tip] Quick Summary
> | Property | Injective | Surjective | Bijective |
> |---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
> | Each output hit at most once? | ✓ | - | ✓ |
> | Every output hit at least once? | - | ✓ | ✓ |
> | $|A|$ vs $|B|$ (finite sets) | $|A| \le |B|$ | $|A| \ge |B|$ | $|A| = |B|$ |

## Proving Injectivity and Surjectivity

> [!abstract] How to Prove or Disprove
> | Goal | Method |
> |---|---|
> | **Prove injective** | Assume $f(x) = f(y)$, show $x = y$ |
> | **Disprove injective** | Find $x \neq y$ with $f(x) = f(y)$ (counterexample) |
> | **Prove surjective** | For arbitrary $y \in B$, find $x \in A$ with $f(x) = y$ |
> | **Disprove surjective** | Find $y \in B$ with no $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$ |

> [!example] Example: Is $f$ onto?
> $f: \{a,b,c,d\} \to \{1,2,3\}$ with $f(a) = 3, f(b) = 2, f(c) = 1, f(d) = 3$.
> 
> **Yes**, $f$ is onto - every element of $\{1,2,3\}$ is an image: $1 = f(c), 2 = f(b), 3 = f(a)$.
> 
> But if the codomain were $\{1,2,3,4\}$, then $f$ is NOT onto because $4$ has no preimage.

> [!example] Example: Is $f(x) = x^2$ from $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ onto?
> **No.** There's no integer $x$ with $x^2 = -1$ (or any negative number).
> So negative integers in the codomain are never hit.

## Inverse Functions

> [!definition] Inverse Function
> If $f: A \to B$ is a **bijection**, then its **inverse** $f^{-1}: B \to A$ is defined by:
> $$f^{-1}(y) = x \iff f(x) = y$$

> [!warning] Inverse exists only for bijections
> If $f$ is not injective: some $y$ has multiple preimages → $f^{-1}(y)$ is ambiguous.
> If $f$ is not surjective: some $y$ has no preimage → $f^{-1}(y)$ is undefined.

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
    % Set A
    \draw[thick, fill=blue!8] (-2,0) ellipse (1 and 1.5);
    \node[font=\small, blue] at (-2,1.8) {$A$};
    \node[font=\small] (a1) at (-2,0.7) {$a$};
    \node[font=\small] (a2) at (-2,0) {$b$};
    \node[font=\small] (a3) at (-2,-0.7) {$c$};
    % Set B
    \draw[thick, fill=red!8] (2,0) ellipse (1 and 1.5);
    \node[font=\small, red!70!black] at (2,1.8) {$B$};
    \node[font=\small] (b1) at (2,0.7) {$1$};
    \node[font=\small] (b2) at (2,0) {$2$};
    \node[font=\small] (b3) at (2,-0.7) {$3$};
    % f arrows (top)
    \draw[->, thick, blue!60] ([yshift=2pt]a1.east) to[bend left=15] node[above, font=\footnotesize] {$f$} ([yshift=2pt]b2.west);
    \draw[->, thick, blue!60] ([yshift=2pt]a2.east) to[bend left=10] ([yshift=2pt]b3.west);
    \draw[->, thick, blue!60] ([yshift=2pt]a3.east) to[bend left=5] ([yshift=2pt]b1.west);
    % f^{-1} arrows (bottom)
    \draw[->, thick, red!60] ([yshift=-2pt]b1.west) to[bend left=15] node[below, font=\footnotesize] {$f^{-1}$} ([yshift=-2pt]a3.east);
    \draw[->, thick, red!60] ([yshift=-2pt]b2.west) to[bend left=10] ([yshift=-2pt]a1.east);
    \draw[->, thick, red!60] ([yshift=-2pt]b3.west) to[bend left=5] ([yshift=-2pt]a2.east);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

> [!example] Inverse Examples
> **Example 1:** $f: \{a,b,c\} \to \{1,2,3\}$ with $f(a) = 2, f(b) = 3, f(c) = 1$.
> 
> This is a bijection, so: $f^{-1}(1) = c$, $f^{-1}(2) = a$, $f^{-1}(3) = b$.
> 
> **Example 2:** $f: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ with $f(x) = x + 1$.
> 
> Bijection ✓ → $f^{-1}(y) = y - 1$.
> 
> **Example 3:** $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $f(x) = x^2$.
> 
> NOT invertible - $f(2) = f(-2) = 4$, so it's not injective.

## Composition of Functions

Composition is just **chaining** functions together - the output of one becomes the input of the next. Think of it like a pipeline: data flows through $g$, then the result flows through $f$.

> [!definition] Composition
> Given $g: A \to B$ and $f: B \to C$, the **composition** $f \circ g: A \to C$ is:
> $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$$
> Read: "f of g of x." Apply $g$ first, then $f$.

> [!tip] WHY does order matter?
> The notation $f \circ g$ reads "$f$ after $g$" - but you evaluate from the **inside out**. The rightmost function ($g$) runs first.
> 
> Think of it like getting dressed: "jacket after shirt" means put the shirt on first, then the jacket. Reversing the order gives a very different (and awkward) result.
> 
> For $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x + 1$:
> - $f \circ g$: first add 1, **then** square → $(x+1)^2$
> - $g \circ f$: first square, **then** add 1 → $x^2 + 1$
> 
> Completely different! $(2+1)^2 = 9$ vs $2^2 + 1 = 5$.

```tikz
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
    % Three sets
    \draw[thick, fill=blue!8] (-3,0) circle (0.9);
    \node[blue, font=\small] at (-3,1.2) {$A$};
    \node[font=\small] (a) at (-3,0) {$a$};
    
    \draw[thick, fill=green!8] (0,0) circle (0.9);
    \node[green!60!black, font=\small] at (0,1.2) {$B$};
    \node[font=\small] (b) at (0,0) {$g(a)$};
    
    \draw[thick, fill=red!8] (3,0) circle (0.9);
    \node[red!70!black, font=\small] at (3,1.2) {$C$};
    \node[font=\small] (c) at (3,0) {$f(g(a))$};
    
    % Arrows
    \draw[->, thick] (a.east) -- (b.west) node[midway, above, font=\footnotesize] {$g$};
    \draw[->, thick] (b.east) -- (c.west) node[midway, above, font=\footnotesize] {$f$};
    \draw[->, thick, dashed] (a.south) to[bend right=30] node[midway, below, font=\footnotesize] {$f \circ g$} (c.south);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

> [!warning] Composition is NOT commutative
> $f \circ g \neq g \circ f$ in general!

> [!example] Composition Example 1
> $f(x) = x^2$, $g(x) = 2x + 1$
> 
> - $f(g(x)) = f(2x+1) = (2x+1)^2$
> - $g(f(x)) = g(x^2) = 2x^2 + 1$
> 
> Different results. Order matters.

> [!example] Composition Example 2 - Discrete Sets
> $g: \{a,b,c\} \to \{a,b,c\}$ with $g(a) = b, g(b) = c, g(c) = a$
> $f: \{a,b,c\} \to \{1,2,3\}$ with $f(a) = 3, f(b) = 2, f(c) = 1$
> 
> **Working out $f \circ g$:**
> - $(f \circ g)(a) = f(g(a)) = f(b) = 2$
> - $(f \circ g)(b) = f(g(b)) = f(c) = 1$
> - $(f \circ g)(c) = f(g(c)) = f(a) = 3$
> 
> Note: $g \circ f$ is **not defined** because the range of $f$ is $\{1,2,3\}$, which isn't a subset of the domain of $g$.

> [!example] Composition Example 3
> $f(x) = 2x + 3$, $g(x) = 3x + 2$ (both $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$)
> 
> - $(f \circ g)(x) = f(3x + 2) = 2(3x+2) + 3 = 6x + 7$
> - $(g \circ f)(x) = g(2x + 3) = 3(2x+3) + 2 = 6x + 11$

## Graphs of Functions

> [!definition] Graph of a Function
> The **graph** of $f: A \to B$ is the set of ordered pairs:
> $$\{(a, b) \mid a \in A \text{ and } f(a) = b\}$$

For functions on integers, the graph is a set of isolated points. For real-valued functions, it's a continuous curve.

## Floor and Ceiling Functions

Two of the most useful functions in discrete math and computer science.

> [!definition] Floor and Ceiling
> - **Floor:** $\lfloor x \rfloor$ = largest integer $\le x$ (rounds down)
> - **Ceiling:** $\lceil x \rceil$ = smallest integer $\ge x$ (rounds up)

> [!example] Floor and Ceiling Examples
> | $x$ | $\lfloor x \rfloor$ | $\lceil x \rceil$ |
> |:---:|:---:|:---:|
> | $3.5$ | $3$ | $4$ |
> | $-1.5$ | $-2$ | $-1$ |
> | $7$ | $7$ | $7$ |
> | $-0.1$ | $-1$ | $0$ |
> 
> When $x$ is already an integer, $\lfloor x \rfloor = \lceil x \rceil = x$.

> [!tip] WHY is $\lfloor -1.5 \rfloor = -2$ and not $-1$?
> This trips people up. Floor means "round *down*" on the number line, not "toward zero."
> 
> Picture the number line: $\cdots \quad -3 \quad -2 \quad \underset{\uparrow}{-1.5} \quad -1 \quad 0 \quad 1 \quad \cdots$
> 
> "Down" means to the **left**. The nearest integer to the left of $-1.5$ is $-2$, not $-1$.
> 
> Similarly, ceiling means "round *up*" (to the right): $\lceil -1.5 \rceil = -1$ because $-1$ is the nearest integer to the **right** of $-1.5$.
> 
> **Shortcut:** For negative numbers, floor goes *away* from zero, ceiling goes *toward* zero.

> [!abstract] Useful Floor/Ceiling Properties
> ($n$ is an integer, $x$ is a real number)
> 
> **Characterizations:**
> - $\lfloor x \rfloor = n \iff n \le x < n + 1$
> - $\lceil x \rceil = n \iff n - 1 < x \le n$
> 
> **Bounds:**
> $$x - 1 < \lfloor x \rfloor \le x \le \lceil x \rceil < x + 1$$
> 
> **Negation:**
> - $\lfloor -x \rfloor = -\lceil x \rceil$
> - $\lceil -x \rceil = -\lfloor x \rfloor$
> 
> **Integer addition:**
> - $\lfloor x + n \rfloor = \lfloor x \rfloor + n$
> - $\lceil x + n \rceil = \lceil x \rceil + n$

## Factorial Function

> [!definition] Factorial
> $f: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z}^+$, defined by $f(n) = n!$
> 
> $$n! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots (n-1) \cdot n$$
> 
> Special case: $0! = 1$ (by convention).

> [!example] Factorial Examples
> | $n$ | $n!$ |
> |:---:|---:|
> | $0$ | $1$ |
> | $1$ | $1$ |
> | $2$ | $2$ |
> | $3$ | $6$ |
> | $4$ | $24$ |
> | $5$ | $120$ |
> | $6$ | $720$ |
> | $10$ | $3{,}628{,}800$ |
> | $20$ | $2{,}432{,}902{,}008{,}176{,}640{,}000$ |
> 
> Factorials grow **extremely fast**.

> [!info] Stirling's Approximation
> For large $n$:
> $$n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n} \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n$$
> 
> The $\sim$ means $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n} = 1$.

## Exercises

> [!example] Exercise 1 - Bijection Check
> Determine whether each function is a bijection from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$:
> 
> **(a)** $f(x) = 2x + 1$
> - **Injective?** $2x_1 + 1 = 2x_2 + 1 \implies x_1 = x_2$ ✓
> - **Surjective?** For any $y \in \mathbb{R}$, solve $y = 2x+1 \implies x = (y-1)/2 \in \mathbb{R}$ ✓
> - **Answer: Yes, it's a bijection.**
> 
> **(b)** $f(x) = x^2 + 1$
> - **Injective?** $f(1) = f(-1) = 2$, but $1 \neq -1$ ✗
> - **Answer: Not a bijection** (fails injectivity).

> [!example] Exercise 2 - Composition
> Find $f \circ g$ and $g \circ f$ where $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ and $g(x) = x + 2$:
> 
> - $(f \circ g)(x) = f(x+2) = (x+2)^2 + 1 = x^2 + 4x + 5$
> - $(g \circ f)(x) = g(x^2+1) = (x^2+1) + 2 = x^2 + 3$
