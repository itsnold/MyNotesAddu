## I. Learning How to See

Art requires **active viewing**: you can't just glance at something and call it a day. Primitive humans had to really *observe* nature to survive, but modern humans? We're bombarded by images everywhere, and it makes us visually numb.

> [!tip] Seeing vs. Looking
> Artists notice details and relationships that most people completely miss. Training yourself to actually *see* is step one.

---

### The Mona Lisa (Leonardo da Vinci)

> [!example]+ Mona Lisa: Why is this thing so famous?
> ![[attachments/mona_lisa.png]]
> *Leonardo da Vinci, c. 1503–1519*
> 
> Okay so this is considered the most famous painting in Western art. But *why*?
> 
> > [!info]+ What makes it a masterpiece
> > | Aspect | What's going on |
> > |--------|-----------------|
> > | **Lifelike Quality** | People back then were shocked. It looked like actual flesh |
> > | **Sfumato** | Italian for "soft mist of a fountain". Edges around the eyes and mouth are blurred so your imagination fills in the expression |
> > | **Composition** | The background horizon lines don't match on left and right, which makes her shoulder seem to shift when you move |
> > | **The Smile** | Because of sfumato, the smile changes depending on where you look |
> 
> > [!quote]+ Fun fact
> > Got stolen in 1911 by Vincenzo Peruggia. Ironically, the theft is what made it world-famous. Before that, it was just another painting in the Louvre.

> [!definition]+ Terms to know
> **Sfumato**: Painting technique with soft, hazy transitions. Think of it like applying blur in Photoshop but with paint.
> 
> **Sprezzatura**: Renaissance term for studied aloofness, making something difficult look effortless. The aristocratic "I woke up like this" vibe.

## II. Art and Culture

Different cultures decide what's "valuable" in art differently.

### Japanese Art Classification

> [!info]+ How Japan organizes art value
> | Classification | What it means |
> |----------------|---------------|
> | **National Treasures** | Top tier. Can't sell them, can't send them abroad. |
> | **Important Cultural Properties** | Still significant, just below National Treasures |
> | **Living National Treasures** | Title for famous artists/craftspeople who are still alive |

## III. The Power to Bring Faith to Life

Art takes abstract beliefs and makes them *physical*. You can't see "fertility" or "the afterlife", but you can carve them.

### Prehistoric Art

> [!example]+ Venus of Willendorf
> ![[attachments/venus_of_willendorf.png]]
> *c. 25,000 BCE — About 4.4 inches tall*
> 
> - Exaggerated breasts, hips, belly, clearly emphasizing fertility
> - Probably a **fertility charm** or earth mother figure
> - No face, maybe because it's meant to be universal, not a specific person
> - This isn't decoration. It's magic for survival.

> [!example]+ Cave Paintings at Lascaux
> ![[attachments/lascaux_cave.png]]
> *c. 17,000 BCE — Lascaux, France*
> 
> - Bulls, horses, deer — painted deep inside caves, not in living areas
> - These weren't for decoration. Probably **hunting magic**: draw the animal, control the animal
> - The fact that they're hidden suggests ritual, not art galleries

---

### Tribal Art

> [!example]+ African Ceremonial Masks
> ![[attachments/african_mask.png]]
> *Traditional African Mask*
> 
> Here's the thing: when you see these in a museum, they're just wooden objects on a pedestal. But in their actual context, they're worn during dances and rituals. The mask *becomes* the ancestor or spirit. Totally different experience.

> [!definition]+ Shamanism
> Belief system (like in Inuit cultures) where everything has a spirit. Shamans travel between worlds, and masks/art objects help them do it.

---

### Religious Architecture

> [!example]+ Notre Dame Cathedral
> ![[attachments/notre_dame.png]]
> *Paris, 1163–1345 — Gothic Architecture*
> 
> - Those vertical lines aren't just aesthetic. They pull your eyes (and soul) upward
> - Stained glass floods the space with colored light
> - The whole building is basically theology in stone: "look up, heaven is there"

## IV. Art Represents Ideals

Art shows what a culture thinks perfection looks like.

### Greek Art

> [!example]+ Hermes with the Infant Dionysus
> ![[attachments/hermes_dionysus.png]]
> *Praxiteles, c. 340 BCE*
> 
> - This is the Greek ideal: perfect balance between body and mind
> - Looks realistic, but it's actually *improved*: no flaws, perfect proportions
> - **Idealism vs. Realism**: Greeks showed humans as they *should* be, not as they actually are

> [!quote] John Keats
> *"Beauty is truth, truth beauty"*

## V. Art as a Declaration of Power

Rulers use art to say "I'm in charge and don't you forget it."

> [!example]+ Henry VIII (Hans Holbein)
> ![[attachments/henry_viii.png]]
> *Hans Holbein the Younger, 1537*
> 
> - Wide stance, direct stare, ridiculous amount of jewels
> - Everything about this says "I am massive and intimidating"
> - Pure propaganda — this is how Henry wanted to be seen

> [!example]+ Head of Mussolini (Renato Bertelli)
> ![[attachments/mussolini_head.png]]
> *Renato Bertelli, 1933 — "Continuous Profile"*
> 
> - 360-degree rotating head sculpture
> - Message: "Il Duce sees everything, from every angle"
> - Uses **Futurism**, the art movement obsessed with speed, machines, and modernity
> - Art as fascist propaganda, basically

> [!tip]+ Emperor Jahangir (Mughal Art)
> ![[attachments/emperor_jahangir.png]]
> Mughal paintings showed the emperor with a halo and hourglass: divine right + desire for immortality. Same energy as European divine right of kings.

## VI. The Power to Change Beliefs & Shock

### Social Commentary

> [!example]+ The Liberation of Aunt Jemima (Betye Saar)
> ![[attachments/aunt_jemima.png]]
> *Betye Saar, 1972*
> 
> - Mixed media assemblage using a Mammy figure as the central object
> - Holds a rifle in one hand and a broom in the other
> - Small framed image of a Mammy with white child at the bottom
> - Aunt Jemima branding repeated in the background
> - Takes a racist stereotype and *weaponizes* it, transforms the symbol of oppression into armed resistance

---

### The Power to Shock

> [!example]+ L.H.O.O.Q. (Marcel Duchamp)
> ![[attachments/lhooq.png]]
> *Marcel Duchamp, 1919*
> 
> - It's a postcard of the Mona Lisa with a mustache drawn on it
> - **Dadaism** in action: "your sacred masterpiece? I just defaced it. What are you gonna do about it?"
> - The title is a French pun that sounds like "Elle a chaud au cul" (she has a hot ass). Very mature, Marcel.

## VII. The Power to Touch Emotions

> [!example]+ Vietnam Veterans Memorial (Maya Lin)
> ![[attachments/vietnam_memorial.png]]
> *Maya Lin, 1982 — Washington, D.C.*
> 
> - V-shaped black granite wall, cut into the earth
> - Was **super controversial** at first. People wanted heroic statues, not this
> 
> > [!success]+ Why it works
> > - The polished surface reflects you: the living face the dead
> > - Names are listed chronologically, not alphabetically, so you have to *search*
> > - Became a place for mourning, healing, leaving objects

## VIII. The Power to Awaken Senses

> 
> **Ansel Adams**: His Yosemite photos make you feel the grandeur and almost spiritual weight of nature.
> 
> **Imogen Cunningham**: Close-ups of leaves and plants that look almost abstract. Teaches you to see patterns you'd normally walk past.

## IX. The Power to Transform the Ordinary

> [!example]+ Urban Landscape (Zhan Wang)
> ![[attachments/urban_landscape.png]]
> *Zhan Wang*
> 
> - A city skyline built entirely out of **stainless steel kitchenware**: pots, pans, woks
> - Commentary on urbanization: the familiar made strange

> [!example]+ The Gates (Christo and Jeanne-Claude)
> ![[attachments/the_gates.png]]
> *Christo and Jeanne-Claude, 2005 — Central Park, NYC*
> 
> - 7,503 saffron fabric gates along 23 miles of paths
> - Turned a grey winter park into a golden procession
> - The 26 years it took to get permission? Part of the art.

> [!tip]+ Rachel Whiteread: *House* (1993)
> She filled a demolished house with concrete, casting the *empty space* inside. Made the invisible visible.

## X. The Power for the Artist

Why do artists make things?

> [!example]+ Self-Expression: Frida Kahlo
> ![[attachments/little_deer.png]]
> *The Little Deer (El Venado Herido), 1946*
> 
> - Self-portrait as a deer pierced by arrows
> - She was in constant physical pain from a bus accident. This is how she processed it
> - Art as survival, as therapy

> [!tip]+ Play: Paul Klee
> *Dance You Monster to My Soft Song!* Doodles, whimsy, childlike energy. Sometimes art is just... fun.

> [!example]+ Memory: Marc Chagall
> ![[attachments/i_and_the_village.png]]
> *I and the Village, 1911*
> 
> - His Russian-Jewish childhood as a dreamscape
> - Floating figures, overlapping scenes, impossible perspective
> - Nostalgia as visual experience

---

> [!abstract]+ What to take away from all this
> Art can:
> - Teach you to *see* differently
> - Reflect what a culture values
> - Serve political and spiritual power
> - Challenge and shock
> - Heal and connect
> - Transform the mundane
> - Let artists process their inner world
